# Ocean Freight Economics and Cost Drivers

Ocean freight is the world's dominant mode for international trade by volume, handling over 80% of global merchandise trade. It is the lowest-cost mode per kilogram for large-volume shipments, typically 4–6× cheaper than air freight on the same route. Standard transit times range from 14 to 45 days depending on the origin-destination pair and routing.

## Containerised vs Break-Bulk

The vast majority of ocean freight is containerised. Standard container sizes:
- **20-foot container (TEU):** ~21 tonnes maximum cargo weight, ~33 CBM volume
- **40-foot container (FEU):** ~26 tonnes, ~67 CBM
- **FCL (Full Container Load):** Shipper books entire container. Lower per-unit cost for large volumes.
- **LCL (Less than Container Load):** Cargo consolidated with other shippers. Higher per-CBM rate, but accessible for small volumes.

For health commodities, FCL is used for country-level programme replenishment (large orders), LCL for smaller supplementary orders or pilot deliveries.

## Typical Cost Range

Ocean freight rates are volatile and lane-specific. Published spot rates on major indices:
- **Asia–Europe:** USD 1,000–6,000/FEU depending on market conditions (historically USD 1,500; spiked to USD 14,000 during 2021 supply chain disruptions)
- **Europe/US–Sub-Saharan Africa:** USD 1,500–4,500/FEU spot
- **Per kg equivalent (for reference):** typically USD 0.05–0.40/kg, vs USD 3–7/kg for air

## Key Cost Components

1. **Ocean freight rate:** Base rate per TEU or FEU for the lane, set by carriers.
2. **Bunker Adjustment Factor (BAF):** Fuel surcharge. Fluctuates with marine fuel (bunker oil) prices.
3. **Peak Season Surcharge (PSS):** Carrier-applied during high-demand periods (pre-Christmas, Q3 Asian export surge).
4. **Port congestion surcharge:** Applied during terminal backlogs.
5. **Terminal handling charges (THC):** Port-of-origin and port-of-destination fees.
6. **Documentation:** Bill of lading (B/L) preparation, shipper's export declaration.
7. **Inland transport:** Trucking from supplier to origin port; port-to-warehouse at destination.

## Ocean Freight in the USAID Dataset

Ocean freight represents a small share (approximately 3–4%) of USAID health commodity shipments. It is used for: (1) non-urgent bulk commodity replenishment (e.g. condoms, malaria bed nets), (2) heavy equipment or large volumes where airfreight cost is prohibitive, (3) coastal or island destinations with good port access. Landlocked PEPFAR countries (Uganda, Zambia, Malawi, Zimbabwe) are rarely served by ocean freight for final delivery — ocean freight to a coastal hub (Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Beira) followed by inland road transport is the typical multimodal approach.

## UNCTAD Freight Rate Benchmarks

The UNCTAD-World Bank GTCDIT dataset (2016–2021) provides bilateral ocean freight rates (ad-valorem % and USD/kg) by country pair. For ocean mode, UNCTAD rates reflect median trade-weighted costs across all commodities for the lane, providing a structural benchmark. The rates are not time-series predictions — they reflect structural lane cost intensity.
